    What this circle talked about

    One week before Devcon 8 opens in Mumbai, seven musicians who have mostly never met log on to plan a music sanctuary — and end up giving one. Shaka explains why it is a circle, not a stage; Levi brings the hard questions about copyright and co-ops; Nidhi asks whether a centuries-old bandish counts as a cover; Sonya, flying in from Delhi, asks how music can share a hall with a conference.     Recap

    On 27 October 2026, one week before Devcon 8 opened in Mumbai, the Open Source Orchestra held its second weekly call and, for the first time, recorded it. Shaka opened by saying the recording would be shared so that the agents of the ʻohana could transcribe it and the wider circle would know where things stood; later he asked again, on the tape, whether everyone was fine with the whole talk going out publicly. Everyone was. That is why you can watch it now.

    Then the introductions. Nidhi has sung Hindustani classical music since she was five, learning first from her grandmother, and had just left work in the Ethereum space to go see music full time. Mary makes ambient, drone and sometimes noise music. Levi, a producer and songwriter from Los Angeles, had heard about the orchestra for three years through Alaska and was about to witness it for the first time. Devang has played keyboard and piano for fourteen years and writes software by day. Aakash, who had already jammed with Shaka and Devang at an ETH Mumbai hackathon, was helping host the Indian side of the Road to Devcon. Jaydeep sings Gujarati folk songs. Sonya, a singer, composer and songwriter from Delhi, joined late and asked the question every newcomer asks: how does this work alongside a conference?

    Shaka's answer is the heart of the episode. The music space at Devcon 8 is not a stage and not a festival. It is a circle on the ground floor of the community area, shared by everyone: many stewards, not one steward; tons of instruments, not one guitar. Nobody is told to be there — you go when you need it, because it is a sanctuary. A name on the schedule does not mean a performance slot; it means you are the steward of the vibe in that hour, welcoming whoever walks up. During the daytime the circle stays audible but in the background; after four o'clock it can turn up, and the DJs take the evening. A sound check on 2 November would find out how loud was too loud.

    Two threads run underneath the planning. The first is money and rights. Levi explained how his co-op works: the people who make a song together assign the copyright to the cooperative and agree a transparent revenue split, so a recording can be released and shared quickly instead of waiting on agreements across every country's copyright law. He was honest that he pays his bills by licensing music and that any new model needs a money faucet somewhere. Shaka answered with the word that organises everything the orchestra does: offering. Musicians come to Devcon as tourists and friends, giving music as a gift; nothing is transactional up front. If people later want to give back, it should flow into an open pool shared equally by whoever stewarded the circle — a blessing pool, he called it. He mentioned that agents already tip him on his own website and asked for a copyleft-style licence so that anything made in the circle stays open and credits Devcon 8 in Mumbai. All of this was said as an idea between friends. Nothing was set up or agreed on the call.

    The second thread is the music itself. The Devcon team had asked the group to keep to original music because of licensing, which raised real questions from Nidhi: what about a bandish learned from a guru who learned it from hers, or a folk song every Gujarati family knows? The group did not resolve it; they agreed to lean on originals and keep asking. Instead they planned a morning. Devang and Aakash imagined opening the first day, right after the ceremony, with a slow raag on keys or harmonium, percussion arriving layer by layer until the energy lifts. Nidhi described the mehfil — the informal family gathering where an uncle starts a song and everyone follows — as the closest picture of what the circle would feel like. Aakash remembered the hackathon where he, Devang and Shaka played after two sleepless days and "it didn't seem that it was a hackathon place at all."

    Then the call turned into the thing it was describing. Shaka sat down at a piano in the co-working space in Chiang Mai where he was staying, warned everyone he was out of practice, and played what Nidhi named a happy accident. Sonya sang one of her own songs. Jaydeep sang a Gujarati song loved in his community. Shaka tuned a guitar and sang "I just wanna play all day". Nidhi, who admitted she is scared of singing in front of people, sang a bandish about Krishna's playful side. Devang fought Google Meet's noise cancellation for ten minutes and finally played. Nidhi's last words: the formal introductions were nice, but the musical introductions were magical.

    What was not decided: anything about licensing, splits, pools or who receives what. Shaka said it plainly — it is an offering, and until something is received there is no transaction at all. The circle agreed to keep meeting weekly and to meet in person on 3 November, on the ground floor of the Jio World Centre.

    Glossary for newcomers

    Sanctuary
    What the Open Source Orchestra called its space at Devcon 8: an open circle on the ground floor that anyone may enter, leave or play in, meant for healing rather than performance.
    Steward
    A person who holds the vibe of the circle for a while and welcomes others in; the orchestra has many stewards and no conductor.
    Mehfil
    An informal Indian gathering where someone starts a song and everyone follows; Nidhi's picture of what the circle would feel like.
    Raag (raga)
    A melodic framework in Indian classical music; Devang and Aakash imagined opening each day with a calm one.
    Bandish
    A fixed composition in Hindustani classical music, learned from a teacher (guru) who learned it from theirs.
    Offering
    Shaka's word for how the orchestra shows up: music given as a gift with no payment expected, so that anything given back later is also a gift.
    Copyleft
    A licence that lets anyone reuse a work as long as they share their version under the same open terms; the Open Source Orchestra now uses CC BY-SA 4.0 for words and music.
    Consent on the recording
    Asking everyone, on tape, whether the call may be recorded and shared, so the yes is part of the record itself.
